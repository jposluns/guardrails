Adopt it

Adopt OPFiles

There are two ways in. The recommended path is to let your AI development assistant do the adoption; adopting by hand is the fallback for when you cannot. Both reach the same gated store: machine-written TOML sources, generated human-readable views, and a drift gate that re-renders the views and compares them. This page walks the paths, then documents each opf command one by one, then lays out the manual steps.

Two paths

Two ways in, one gated store

Assistant-driven (recommended)

Point your AI development assistant at this site or the repository and tell it to adopt OPFiles. It reads the standard, inspects your project, proposes a plan, and, on your yes, scaffolds and validates the store for you. This path needs no particular tool.

By hand (fallback)

Create the plain-TOML store, configure your own checks, and work records-first. The standard requires no particular tool, so a hand adopter reaches the same store format and can move to the reference tooling later without changing it.

On the assistant-driven path, your assistant reads the standard, investigates your project, and presents a short plan: what it will create, import, or retire, and how the pieces wire together. It asks one question, yes, no, or let us discuss. On your yes it carries out the adoption, scaffolds and validates the store, wires the records-first process into your project's assistant instructions, and leaves the .working/ store for your review.

The machine-readable entry point it follows, an /llms.txt index, a machine-readable /adopt.md, and a link on the root page, lets an assistant find and follow the adoption steps. Adopting AIQT this way brings OPFiles with it and migrates your existing operational files into the store; adopting OPFiles alone is also supported.

Command reference

The opf commands, one by one

OPFiles names a standard command vocabulary; the opf reference CLI is one implementation of it. A base adopter can use their own tooling instead, or adopt by hand. The standard and the reference suite are both a draft, so this reference documents each command by what it does at source today.

Every command shares one exit contract, which is what makes it usable as a commit or CI check: 0 clean, 1 a finding or drift, and 2 cannot-evaluate, a fail-closed "I could not tell" that is never read as a pass. One command-specific exception sits on top of this: render --write refuses a source-integrity violation with exit 2 and writes nothing, so a source defect that doctor reports as a finding (exit 1) makes render --write exit 2, declining to write over an unsound source rather than exiting 1. Where a command is aimed at a directory that has not adopted OPFiles, the applicability probes (render, doctor, upgrade) report NOT APPLICABLE and exit 0. The seven commands, documented one by one below, are init, render, doctor, import, upgrade, migrate, and sync.

opf init

Purpose. Create a fresh, validated store and its committed pointer in a project that has none, without any git write and without rendering.

How it works. A read-only preflight runs first: it inspects the target for an existing store, an existing pointer, or foreign content already under .working/, and refuses rather than absorbing what it finds. It then builds the machine sources in memory (the manifest, the ID counters, the version ledger, the worklog, and the eleven typed indexes), writes them under .working/toml/, writes the .opf.toml pointer at the product root, and creates a CHANGELOG.md if none exists. Publication is create-only: it writes new paths and does not overwrite an existing one.

Result. A scaffolded store, a committed-store pointer, and a starter changelog if you had none, all left untracked for your review; init does not stage or commit them. See core store files for what the scaffold writes into the manifest.

opf init opf init --root .

opf render

Purpose. Regenerate the store's declared views and check them against their sources, in one of two modes.

How it works. Exactly one of --check or --write is required, so a preview never turns itself into a write; a bare opf render is a usage error. --check is read-only: it re-renders every declared view from its sources and compares the bytes, reporting whether the store is clean, has drifted, or cannot be evaluated, and writing nothing. --write gates on source integrity, not on the whole-store verdict: it gathers the git-derived observations, and a drifted or missing view is its output to regenerate rather than a reason to refuse, so it rewrites the declared views from their sources. Only a source violation (a duplicate id, an untracked store, missing or malformed observations, or an unreadable required input) refuses with exit 2 and writes nothing, printing the source-attributed findings, so a write never reads as a silent no-op.

Result. Under --check, a verdict and no change; under --write, the regenerated view files on disk when source integrity holds, or no change and a source-attributed report of why it withheld.

opf render --check opf render --write

opf doctor

Purpose. Run the whole-store integrity layer read-only and return a single verdict.

How it works. It resolves the store at the product root (or at --root DIR), gathers the git-derived observations, and runs the integrity engine over them. It makes no store change, and its report is a structured per-check verdict map, the findings and cannot-evaluates, and a residual count, never a wall of diff lines. Its coverage spans schema validity, ID uniqueness and counter monotonicity, bidirectional index reconciliation, transition legality, the all-or-none resolution bundle, byte drift for every deterministic view, worklog span tiling and frozen digests, changelog coverage and freeze, archive integrity, the tracked-store requirement, pointer and sync-target agreement, and path containment.

Result. A valid, invalid, or cannot-evaluate verdict with the per-check breakdown, exiting 0, 1, or 2 to match.

opf doctor opf doctor --root .

opf import

Purpose. Bring an existing project's operational content into the store through a staged, reviewed, attributed flow, rather than a silent absorb.

How it works. Exactly one of four modes runs, and each requires an already-initialized store (an unresolved root is a cannot-evaluate that asks you to run opf init first). The modes chain in order:

  • --scan --set FILE: reads a declared set of sources and prints a canonical inventory to stdout; zero writes.
  • --plan --set FILE: stages a candidate run under .working/imports/<run-id>/ and prints the run id and the report path, leaving the originals in place; no live-store write.
  • --review <run-id> --actor NAME with --decisions FILE or --interactive: captures an attributed decision on every fragment and writes the acceptance record; no live-store write (a non-TTY --interactive is refused).
  • --apply <run-id>: promotes an accepted, reviewed run into the live store through a journaled, verified cutover.

Result. An inventory, a staged run id and report, an acceptance record, or a promoted store, one per mode; everything not confidently mapped is quarantined rather than dropped, and the originals stay in place until an --apply promotes a fully reviewed run.

opf import --scan --set sources.toml opf import --plan --set sources.toml opf import --review R --actor "you" --decisions d.toml opf import --apply R

opf upgrade

Purpose. Apply the in-place base-schema upgrade (from spec 1.0.0 to 1.1.0), staged for your own review and merge.

How it works. The upgrade is additive and idempotent. It resolves the store and refuses fail-closed on a store above the tooling's spec version, on a hand-edited or comment-bearing manifest or counters, on a held lease, or on a store that is unclean over the paths it will write. Before any write it claims a single-writer lease and holds it across the change, applies exactly the allowed schema delta as a canonical model regeneration (not a textual edit), renders the declared views, and requires a full doctor-valid before offering the change. It never commits: you review and merge. A store already at the target version is a byte no-op, still requiring doctor-valid.

Result. The upgraded store staged in your working tree and left uncommitted for your branch-and-merge; a preflight refusal (the fail-closed checks above: a store above the tooling's spec, a hand-edited or comment-bearing manifest or counters, a held lease, or a store unclean over the paths it writes) stops before any write and changes nothing, while a failure once the additive rewrite has begun (at the render or the final doctor) leaves the staged changes in place for a recorded, store-subtree-scoped recovery against the committed HEAD, not a no-op.

opf upgrade opf upgrade --root .

opf migrate

Purpose. Relocate the whole tracked store to a target location, its git history and worklog preserved, as one recorded, verified operation.

How it works. It runs under the store consistency contract and the single-writer lease, refusing to start from a stale, ahead, or divergent store. It validates the target before any push: the destination is confirmed to be the recorded, intended target rather than inherited from ambient state, so a store is not pushed to an unexpected place. It preserves the store's git history into the destination and the worklog and its archive byte for byte, and keeps the generated public deliverables (CHANGELOG.md and VERSION) in the product repository, rewiring the render targets so future renders keep writing them there. It re-points the committed pointer and the manifest's recorded sync target in the same change, then verifies the destination before retiring the source: the relocated store is cloned back and reconciled (ledgers, indexes, counters, and worklog digests) before the old location is removed or archived, and the removal is recorded.

Result. The store at its new location with the pointer, the manifest's sync target, and the actual remote in agreement; the public deliverables still in the product repository; and a worklog entry naming both the old and the new location. Migration is symmetric: in-repo to a companion repository, companion to in-repo, or anywhere to anywhere, repeatably, each move one recorded, verified, history-preserving operation.

opf migrate --store dir:../product-ops-store opf migrate --store github:acme/product-ops

opf sync

Purpose. Reconcile the store with its sync target, the surfaced pull-and-push step of the store consistency contract.

How it works. The consistency contract holds the store nothing-stale, nothing-ahead against its target. Where the local store is behind the target, opf sync fast-forwards it to current; where it is ahead (local commits not yet pushed, the lease still held by the resuming holder, and no divergent remote side), it pushes the pending commits as an authorized reconciliation. It runs as its own surfaced step and is not folded silently into another operation, so a pull or a push is a deliberate act you can see. A divergence (unsynced commits on two systems) is not auto-merged: opf sync surfaces it and halts for you, because a textual merge of the store's TOML records could mangle the very records the standard protects.

Result. The store reconciled to a known-consistent state with its target, fast-forwarded to current or with its pending local commits pushed, or a surfaced halt on a divergence for you to resolve by hand.

opf sync opf sync --root .

Adopt by hand

The manual path, step by step

OPFiles specifies formats, layout, naming, lifecycle, and enforcement posture, and requires no particular tool: the specification states that a project can conform with hand-maintained files and its own checks. Adopting by hand gives you the records-first discipline today, and you can move to the reference tooling later without changing the store format.

The shape of the manual path is short: create the store (either run opf init to scaffold it, or write the files by hand from the templates below), commit it, run opf render --write to generate the views, and then work records-first. Conformance you assert by hand is self-asserted, and you should say so wherever you claim it, because no validator has checked it for you yet. The steps below fill that in.

Step 1

The minimal store

Create the machine store at .working/toml/ in your repository, with these files:

  • manifest.toml: the control document and discovery marker. It declares standard = "opf" (the exact discovery token) in its [opf] table, along with the base spec_version you conform to, the storage layout, and the enforcement posture.
  • counters.toml: the per-namespace ID high-water marks, so an ID is never reused.
  • version.toml: the version and release ledger (numbers and digests only, never prose).
  • worklog.toml: the durable, append-only operational record. It is the exempt ledger, not a typed index.
  • Eleven typed indexes, one per baseline record type: backlog_item, done, finding, pending_decision, autonomous_decision, maintainer_decision, preference_pattern, block, handoff, reference, and contribution, each as <type>.index.toml (empty to start).

Then write the committed pointer .opf.toml at your product repository root, so the store resolves from a stable location. A [profiles.aiqt] table is optional and is ignored by base-only tooling; a base adopter need not add it.

A minimal manifest, illustrative and drawn from the specification's own example (see the standard for the normative shape, and core store files for every table it can carry):

.working/toml/manifest.toml
[opf] standard = "opf" # discovery token; exact value required spec_version = "1.1.0" # the base spec version this store conforms to layout = "inline" # "inline" or "per-record" posture = "warn" # "off", "warn", or "required" (section 11) import_status = "none" # "none", "partial", or "complete"

Step 2

The layout, and the one rule of adoption

Machine source lives under .working/toml/; generated human-readable views live at .working/ top level; the curated CHANGELOG.md and the generated VERSION live at the product repository root.

<product repository root>/
  .opf.toml                 committed store pointer
  CHANGELOG.md              curated public changelog
  VERSION                   rendered from version.toml
  .working/
    BACKLOG.md FINDINGS.md DECISIONS.md WORKLOG.md ...   generated views (UPPERCASE)
    toml/
      manifest.toml counters.toml version.toml worklog.toml
      backlog_item.index.toml  and its ten sibling indexes

One rule of adoption carries the whole convention: lowercase files are machine source you change through tooling or review; UPPERCASE files are generated or published deliverables you read and never hand-edit. The curated CHANGELOG.md is the one you edit, and only through its publish flow. Once the files exist, commit them, then run opf render --write (or your own generator) to produce the views from their sources.

Step 3

Working records-first

Treat the store as the source of truth: a decision, finding, or completion that is not recorded did not happen. Append a worklog entry per change; keep the backlog, findings, and decisions in their typed files; and regenerate the views rather than editing them. Records carry a typed envelope (an ID, type, status, title, timestamps read from the clock, an actor, links, and captured references), IDs are never reused, and records are corrected before release and frozen after it, superseded by new records that link the old rather than deleted.

Step 4

The release triad by hand

At a release, record the release and the worklog span it covers as a [[release]] row in version.toml; draft the summary from that span; curate it by hand; publish it into CHANGELOG.md; and record the freeze digest for the published entry. Old summaries can be rolled up into range summaries later, because the details always survive in the worklog, so re-rolling or re-wording stays safe.

Step 5

Bring your own checks

Even before you run the reference validator, you can assert the standard's key invariants yourself. A hand adopter can check that the store is a tracked git repository (an untracked store is a hard failure, not a warning); that generated views match a fresh render; that worklog spans tile the released versions with no gap or overlap; and that the changelog range tiles every released version, with published entries unchanged since their recorded digest. These are the checks the integrity layer performs when the tooling runs; asserting them by hand keeps you honest in the meantime.

The honest limits

What by-hand adoption does not give you

Without the validation tooling, your conformance is self-asserted and should say so. Digest freezing and span tiling are your own discipline until a validator checks them. Some residuals are part of the standard itself and no adopter escapes them: the freeze gate cannot judge whether prose is accurate, range coverage cannot judge summary quality, and the unreleased worklog tail rests on review rather than machine freezing. The disclosure page states these limits plainly.
