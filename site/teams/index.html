Ask two colleagues what their AI assistant means by "done" and you will get two answers. Today the quality of your team's AI-assisted work depends on who did it and which tool they used. AIQT offers a single governance standard intended to reach across Claude, ChatGPT, and Copilot, so a colleague on one assistant is meant to work to the same rules as a colleague on another. That cross-assistant reach is the intent, not a verified result yet: the evidence page marks every platform pending, with ChatGPT, Gemini, and Copilot not yet tested. Adoption today is human-led: each person adds AIQT to their own assistant, under a rollout your team runs. Team features, such as management and monitoring, performance metrics, and guardrail bundles, are planned, with no dates yet, and not available today.

Today

What a shared standard gives your team today

One definition of "done", intended for every assistant on the team, with a record behind it. Most teams already have people using AI assistants, each with their own habits and their own idea of what "done" means; AIQT gives them one ordering, decided in advance.

Consistency you did not have to police

The quality-versus-speed argument is settled once, in advance, instead of on every deadline. Every assistant on the team is meant to work to the same ordering, so the call is already made before the pressure arrives.

Sign-off backed by evidence you can check

The standard asks the assistant to trace claims to sources, say what a guardrail caught, and log overrides. With the chat Skill (1.0.6, in the AIQT 1.0.5 release), that record lives in the conversation itself: the stated sources, the named catches, and the logged overrides you can scroll back to. Repository-level records are part of the 1.2.0 design, in development. Reporting across a team is planned, as performance metrics.

A standard designed to survive tool changes

The standard is designed to be portable across assistants and toolchains, so that swapping Claude for ChatGPT, or adding Copilot, keeps the same rules and your team's definition of "done" is not locked to any vendor. That is the design intent, not a tested result: ChatGPT and Copilot are not yet tested.

Gaps turned into guardrails

When the assistant's own gap lets an issue through, Rule 5 asks it to propose a guardrail so the issue should not recur, and to draft it if you ask. Sending the lesson back to AIQT, so one team's lesson can become every team's guardrail, is part of the 1.2.0 design: an opt-in step, Rule 5's voluntary Guardrail-Seed give-back, separate from the software licence.

Teams that write code have a version in development: the development assistant (1.2.0) is designed to install the same standard into a project, keep to its own directory, and integrate with your CI. It is not available yet.

Not available today

What does not exist yet

Said plainly, so you can plan around it.

  • Central deployment of the Skill to a team.
  • Technical enforcement of the standard: it is a behavioural standard the assistant follows, not a control that blocks a model.
  • A shared or aggregate record across people.
  • Admin controls, policy management, and cross-team reporting.
  • Commercial support and service levels.

For what is planned, see the planned features below: management and monitoring, performance metrics, and guardrail bundles. They have no dates yet.

Start a team pilot

How to evaluate AIQT with your team

Today's team path is a human-led pilot: a small group, the assistants they already use, and a few weeks of normal work. Here is one way to run it.

  1. Choose a small group and the assistants they already use (Claude, ChatGPT, or Copilot).
  2. Each person installs the AIQT chat Skill (1.0.6, in the AIQT 1.0.5 release) and confirms that it is on.
  3. Run two or three weeks of normal work, noting a few observations: unsupported factual claims, completion claims without a check, missed requirements, clarification questions asked, overrides, rework, and reviewer confidence.
  4. Compare against the period before, on the same observations.
  5. Decide: keep it, extend it, or remove it.

Questions, or want to share what you found? Open a GitHub discussion (opens in a new tab). There is no sales or support team yet; this is an open project, and that is the honest state of it. The evidence page collects the release, limitations, and documented cases.

Planned

What is coming for teams and enterprises

Everything in this section is planned, with no dates yet. None of it ships today. What you can use today is the 1.0.5 chat assistant, which each person adds to their own assistant, and the pack, which is open to read in full on GitHub. The future plans page lists the same plans.

We share these plans so you can tell us whether they would help your organization. Open a GitHub discussion (opens in a new tab) to send your comments.

  1. 1

    Management and monitoring. One view of every project using AIQT: which guardrails each runs, at which version, and where an update is waiting.

  2. 2

    Performance metrics. How often each guardrail fires and what it stopped, per project and per team, so you can see where the standard is earning its place.

  3. 3

    Guardrail bundles. Pre-built sets of guardrails per project type or purpose, curated, versioned, and rolled out by the team or enterprise, so every project starts from the same agreed baseline.

For a team lead, these would give one place to see what the guardrails stopped and how the standard is holding across the work. For an organization, they would let the standard be set once, as a bundle, so every team starts from it.

How we decide

How we decide what ships

We move a planned feature into a release only when we can say what it does and point to the evidence behind it, marking what is still pending rather than implying it. That rule is why this page separates what is available today from what is planned: the same standard AIQT holds an assistant to, we hold ourselves to. A claim about the product should trace to evidence, and "available" should mean a check actually ran.

Open source

Open source, portable, and shared

The guardrails are designed to be portable across toolchains, so that adopting the standard leaves your team free to change tools without changing rules. Sending an improvement back is Rule 5's voluntary Guardrail-Seed give-back, separate from the software licence; the route for it, a seed pull request, is part of the 1.2.0 design.
